    Decision 01 — The user

    Decision 01 — The user

    She doesn't distrust the number. She distrusts that she can't check it.

    A project accountant at a mid-market contractor — $50M to $500M in revenue, Procore for the field, Vista or Spectrum for the money, and nobody on staff who writes integrations. That last part is why this whole category exists.

    Mid-meeting, someone asks why job 1412 is over budget. Her AI tool answers in three seconds with a tidy paragraph explaining where the figure came from. Then she opens Vista and rebuilds the number by hand anyway.

    When she's wrong in front of the owner, it's her name on it — not the tool's.

    The reason she rebuilds it
    So what

    The tool didn't save her an afternoon. It added a thing she has to check. Every "show your work" panel that narrates itself makes this worse, not better — it gives her more to read and no more to verify.

    Decision 02 — The workflow we are entering

    Every gate we build removes a step she does by hand.

    A project manager at a general contractor, reconciling two systems that were never designed to agree: the ERP holds sanctioned project status under a PID; the finance contract DB holds the money under an FMS ID. Her whole job is one join nobody guaranteed.

    The eight evals — six in, two out

    Phase H is not an eval — it is the action the eight produce.

    E7 depends entirely on E2

    A duplicate search that runs against one spelling of a vendor finds nothing and reports clean. That dependency is the demo: resolve the vendor, and the duplicate appears. It is also why E2 is the critical-path eval — E7 cannot be graded before it, and E7 is the closing beat.

    What "out of scope" means here

    OutWhy not by Sunday
    E6 · authority + balanceNeeds an authority matrix and a live disbursal balance. Neither exists in open data, and faking either turns a real demo into a rigged one
    E8 · other contractualNeeds waivers, certificates and certified payroll — documents, not tables. The natural next build, and where a document-parsing layer earns its place
    Both stay on the diagram

    Drawn dashed, not deleted. A judge sees the whole workflow and sees exactly where we stopped. A gap you name is credibility; a gap you hide is the thing they find.

    The confidence score is a count, not a feeling

    ActionFires whenConfidence
    ApproveEvery applicable gate passed and the figure reconciled to the source total within tolerancepassed ÷ applicable
    CommentAt least one gate returned cannot evaluate — an unresolved alias, a missing mapping, an undefined statusreports the unevaluable gates by name
    DenyAt least one gate hard-failed — duplicate found, invoice exceeds contract, remit-to changed1.00 — a hard fail is certain
    The rule that makes it honest

    An unevaluable gate is never counted as passed. It leaves the denominator and routes to Comment. A model that says "95% confident" has produced another fabricated receipt — this number is gates passed over gates applicable, and every term is nameable.

    Two steps we deliberately do not automate

    D5 — walk it, or call the superintendent. No system knows what was actually built. H4 — record why, not merely that. The judgment is the approval; we supply the evidence, never the decision.

    Decision 02 — What it costs

    The cost isn't the wrong number. It's the second person who has to produce the right one.

    Two of these are named incidents at named companies. The rest are the ordinary, unbilled cost of a tool nobody can lean on.

    What goes wrongCostHow it shows up

    So what

    Making the answer faster does nothing here — she was never waiting on speed. Making the answer checkable is the entire product. The first time she clicks a receipt and it genuinely reconciles, she stops rebuilding by hand. That's the afternoon back.

    Hours and adoption cost are estimates. The $68,000 and $200,000 are named incidents at named companies and are the only two figures on this page that are quotable as fact.

    Decision 03 — The failure we're fixing

    A wrong number arrives wearing a convincing receipt.

    Watch it happen on real public money. Every figure below is from NYC's own capital budget, reporting period 202605.

    The question

    "What's our total capital budget this period?"

    The answer, in three seconds

    $142,756,981,094

    "Summed total_budget across all projects in the current reporting period, joined to the project schedule on the FMS identifier."

    The query that actually ran 
    SELECT SUM(b.total_budget)
FROM   budget_and_schedule b
JOIN   projects p ON p.pid = b.pid
WHERE  b.reporting_period = '202605'
-- inner join. 2,356 rows have no pid at all.
    Reconciled against the source total
    Reported$142.76B
    Actually in the period$207.59B
    Silently dropped$64.84B · 31.2%

    Every word of that explanation is true except all. The narration was generated by the same model that wrote the query, so the receipt is written by the suspect. Nothing in the pipeline errored. Nothing turned red.

    The build, in one sentence

    We don't grade the answer. We grade the receipt against the query that executed — join keys, filters, and what got dropped — and we refuse to answer when it doesn't tie back.

    Decision 04 — The opportunity

    Everyone at the hackathon builds text-to-SQL. We build the thing that catches it.

    The standard demo picks a friendly question and works. This one opens on the failure — a confident wrong number with a beautiful explanation — and then catches it live. Every judge in the room has been burned by a plausible LLM answer.

    It fits a weekend for an unglamorous reason: there is nothing to integrate.

    In scopeOut
    Read-only queries over public dataAny write path
    Executed-query capture + reconciliationAuth, tenancy, RBAC
    A graded gold set of ~30–50 hard casesProcore / ERP integration
    Refuse-to-answer when the tie-out failsFine-tuning anything
    One screen a controller would recogniseVision / document OCR layer
    The unfair advantage

    The failure mode already exists in the public data. We don't have to fabricate a broken join to demo catching one — which is the difference between a demo and a rigged demo, and a technical judge can tell.

    Decision 05 — The data

    NYC publishes $207B of capital spend — and documents its own broken join key.

    From the dataset description NYC ships with fb86-vt7u, read live today:

    Each row is uniquely identified by its Financial Management Service (FMS) ID… This ID can be universally joined with any OMB dataset that has the same field… FMS IDs and agency projects don't always have a one-to-one relationship (i.e., one project schedule may correlate to multiple FMS IDs, and one FMS may correct to multiple projects with different schedules).

    NYC Open Data · Capital Projects Dashboard · dataset description

    Three claims in one paragraph, and they cannot all be true. The key is unique. Join on it universally. The key is not unique. The dataset's own row label offers a fourth answer — "unique project and budget line combination".

    DatasetIDCarriesKey
    Citywide Budget and Schedulefb86-vt7uThe bridge — budget, spend, phase, schedule, agencyfms_id + pid
    Budget Spend History and Varianceqj5n-h5qpMonthly snapshots. Earliest = original budget; variance = delta vs prior monthfms_id
    Capital Commitment Plan2cmn-uidmCommitted dollars by budget line and funding sourceno fms_id

    Correction carried forward. The prior handoff recorded this family as "all keyed on FMS ID". It isn't — 2cmn-uidm has no fms_id column at all; it keys on budline, projtype and funding. Verified against the live Socrata metadata, not recalled.

    Why this beats a synthetic dataset

    The monthly snapshot trail in qj5n-h5qp is an original-versus-revised budget history — the change-order story in contractor terms, in real money, published by the party that owns it. Pair it with BookSQL for question variety and accounting structure.

    Decision 06 — The proof measured

    Two failures, both already in the data, both invisible to a green pipeline.

    Queried live against fb86-vt7u, reporting period 202605. Not estimated.

    Failure one — the key fans out

    207of 3,063 projects carry more than one FMS ID
    382surplus rows a naive join invents
    $9.93Bsits on those projects
    14FMS IDs on the worst single project (pid 2845)

    Failure two — the join drops a third of the money and says nothing

    2,356rows with no project ID (2,343 distinct FMS IDs)
    $64.84Bof budget on those rows
    31.2%of the period's dollars unjoinable to a project
    $0errors raised when this happens
    The one that matters

    Fan-out double-counts loudly — a total that grows is a total someone questions. The silent drop is the dangerous one: an inner join discards $64.84B and returns a smaller, tidier, entirely credible number. Neither prior handoff had this second failure. The data did.

    Decision 07 — Why this is Shivam's project

    The hard part isn't generating SQL. It's building the ground truth that grades it.

    This is an eval problem end to end, and it is the specific flavour of eval problem that is genuinely hard to fake in a weekend.

    What he already doesWhat it becomes here
    Finance-document parsing — the table top-up heuristic, so numeric questions don't get answered from proseThe same instinct, one layer up: a numeric answer must not be explained by prose. The narration is graded against the query, not accepted as one
    Duplicate detection at portfolio scale — GBM across 32 clients, 50M+ transactions/monthFan-out detection on a join key that the publisher itself says is not unique
    Entity resolution by metric learning — triplet mining, silhouette 0.72 → 0.90Is fms_id = pid the same entity? The dataset says both yes and no
    Interpretability — SHAP, PDP, ELI5 since 2020The receipt itself. Why this number, traced to the rows that produced it

    The engineering excellence he gets to show

    Semantics here are genuinely nasty and no amount of prompt engineering gets past them: committed versus actual versus forecast, retention held back, pending versus approved change orders. A join can be technically valid and financially wrong — so the eval has to catch semantic wrongness, not syntax errors.

    The research surface

    Faithfulness scoring at the level of joins and filters, not vibes: does the explanation describe the query that ran? That's a groundedness eval where the "retrieved evidence" is an execution plan. Very few people at this hackathon will be grading a narration against a query plan.

    Decision 08 — Why this is Isha's project

    A bad join returns a number, not an error. Her job is to make the pipeline stop lying quietly.

    $64.84B disappears from an answer and every health check stays green. This is the silent-default anti-pattern in its natural habitat — and turning silent failure into loud, fast failure is the discipline she already sells.

    GuardWhat it doesFailure mode it kills
    Tie-out, fail closedDoes this figure reconcile to the source total within tolerance? If not, refuse to answer — don't degrade gracefullyConfident wrong totals
    Unmatched-row accountingEvery join reports what it dropped, in rows and in dollars, on the answer itselfThe $64.84B silent drop
    Cardinality contractAssert the expected key relationship before the join; a 1:1 assumption that turns 1:N throwsThe 207-project fan-out
    Provenance envelopeEvery number carries its query, schema version and data watermark"Was the model wrong, or the data stale?" — currently unanswerable

    The objection, answered

    The earlier scoring knocked this idea down on audience recognition, on the grounds that the budget view is its interface — the harness isn't a visible layer beside a recognisable Procore screen, it is the screen.

    That's the argument for it, not against it

    A reliability layer that lives beside the number gets ignored. This one sits where the number is read, so refusing to answer is a product behaviour a controller experiences, not a dashboard she'd have to go look at. Recognition comes from the field names on screen — total_budget, budget_variance, current_phase — not from a screenshot.

    Appendix C — The instruments

    Two instruments, never one number — because an average dilutes every spike.

    InstrumentBehaviour
    FloorBinary, and it blocks. A project is eligible only if it can name a verbatim field, enum or endpoint. Five rows fail. They sink but are not deleted
    PeakRanks on the sum of a project's top two criteria only. Everything below contributes nothing, so one elite spike beats four competent scores

    The five criteria — scored on scarcity, not goodness

    The question is never is this good. It is can another team produce it. RAG plumbing scores low because everyone has it.

    RankCriterionWeightHow it's scored
    1Domain grounding1.00Derived, not judged — five countable sub-signals worth 0–2 each: anchor · index · term count · JTBD grade · quote
    2Isha unique strengths0.85Declared judgment
    3Shivam fit0.70Declared judgment, graded on the rare half of his profile, not the RAG plumbing
    4Construction-tech recognition0.55Declared judgment
    5General-software recognition0.25Declared judgment

    Ceiling is 18.5 — a perfect domain-grounding score plus a perfect single spike.

    Read this before trusting the ranking

    Only criterion 1 is derived from counts. Four of the five are declared judgment. And the ordering of criteria 2 and 3 — Isha 0.85 above Shivam 0.70 — was supplied as a priority, not derived from evidence, yet it is the only thing separating first place from second on a 0.65 margin. The ranking is a useful instrument, not an oracle. It is one of the reasons the selected project was chosen on evidence the ranking never scored.
